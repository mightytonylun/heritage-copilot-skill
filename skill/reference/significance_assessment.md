# Significance Assessment Method

Supports Phase 2 of the `heritage-copilot` skill. Based on the value categories in
the Burra Charter (Australia ICOMOS, 2013), Article 1.2, and its accompanying
Practice Notes. This file paraphrases the method — it does not reproduce Charter
text. See australia.icomos.org for the authoritative document.

---

## Value categories

Assess the place against each. A place may hold several values, or one strongly.

| Value | Asks |
|-------|------|
| **Aesthetic** | Does the place have visual, sensory, or design quality — form, scale, materials, craftsmanship, landmark presence, landscape character? |
| **Historic** | Is the place associated with a person, group, event, phase, activity, or pattern of history? Does its fabric show how things were done? |
| **Scientific / research** | Can the place yield information not available elsewhere — through its fabric, archaeology, or construction? Consider rarity, representativeness, condition, and research potential. |
| **Social** | Is the place held in strong or special regard by a community or cultural group — for spiritual, political, national, or other reasons? |
| **Spiritual** | Does the place hold intangible meanings, sacred associations, or sustain spiritual identity for a group? This includes Aboriginal connections to Country. |

For each value: state present / not present / evidence needed, give the supporting
evidence, and indicate the degree (e.g. of local, state, or national importance) if
the evidence supports it.

---

## Writing the Statement of Significance

A Statement of Significance is a short, plain-language summary. It should answer:

- **What** is significant? (the place, and which elements specifically)
- **Why** is it significant? (which values, and the evidence)
- **How** significant, and to whom? (degree and community)

Keep it to one or two paragraphs. Avoid jargon. Do not list works or condition here —
significance is about meaning and value, not repair state.

---

## Grading significance by element

Phase 4 needs to know which fabric to protect most. Grade each principal element:

| Grade | Meaning | Conservation implication |
|-------|---------|--------------------------|
| **Exceptional** | Rare or outstanding fabric central to the place's significance. | Retain and preserve. Change only with the strongest justification. |
| **High** | Fabric that contributes strongly to significance. | Retain. Repair in preference to replacement. |
| **Moderate** | Fabric that supports significance or is typical of the place. | Retain where practical. Sympathetic change acceptable. |
| **Little** | Fabric that neither adds to nor detracts from significance. | Change acceptable if it does not harm significant fabric. |
| **Intrusive** | Fabric that detracts from significance (poor later additions). | A candidate for removal, if removal does not damage significant fabric. |

---

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Confusing **condition** with **significance**. A deteriorated element can be highly
  significant; a pristine element can be intrusive.
- Confusing **age** with **significance**. Old is not automatically significant, and
  recent fabric can hold social or historic value.
- Assessing only **aesthetic** value because it is the easiest to see. Social and
  spiritual values are often the most important and the least documented.
- Writing significance to justify a predetermined outcome. Assess first, decide after.
