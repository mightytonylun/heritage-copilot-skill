# Traditional Materials and Common Mistakes

Supports Phases 3 and 4 of the `heritage-copilot` skill. Traditional buildings behave
differently from modern ones. Applying modern materials and methods to them — even
with good intentions — is a frequent cause of avoidable damage. This file paraphrases
guidance from the Australia ICOMOS *Conservation Guidelines for Building Surveyors*
(see acknowledgements). It is not structural or specialist advice.

---

## The golden rule

Repair traditional fabric with the **same materials and techniques** used originally,
especially when patching rather than replacing. Patching with modern materials is
usually inappropriate because old and new materials are often incompatible.

Conservation keeps the patina of age. The goal is not to make old fabric look new.

---

## Mortar and masonry

- Before WWI, most masonry was laid in **lime mortar**, deliberately *weaker* than the
  brick or stone. Moisture and salts then decay the mortar joints — which are easily
  repointed — rather than the masonry units.
- **Cement-rich mortar is denser and stronger than the masonry.** Used to repoint
  traditional brick or stone, it traps moisture and soluble salts, which then break
  down the brick/stone face instead (spalling). Cracks also run *through the units*
  rather than the joints.
- Repoint traditional masonry with a **lime-based mortar**, not cement.
- Spalling stone can sometimes be cleaned and rubbed back rather than replaced.

## Damp

- Rising/penetrating damp is usually **excess water**: failed gutters and downpipes,
  blocked drains, soil banked against walls, impervious paving against the building,
  over-watered garden beds. **Eliminate the water source first** — this alone often
  solves the problem.
- **Cement render does not fix rising damp — it makes it worse**, pushing the damp
  higher up the wall. A weak lime-based render is breathable and far better.
- A damp-proof course is a *last resort*, after all water sources are addressed.
- Damp can persist for a long time after the cause is fixed, while the fabric dries.

## Walls and finishes

- Most traditional walls are **solid masonry without a cavity** and stay dry inside
  by letting moisture evaporate through the wall.
- **Oil-based paint and modern liquid membranes stop the wall breathing**, forcing
  moisture to the inside face and worsening damp. On previously painted masonry use
  breathable acrylic or limewash.
- Stone and face brick meant to be exposed should **not be painted** at all.

## Metals

- **Incompatible metals corrode rapidly in contact.** Modern zincalume steel is not
  compatible with lead flashings, or with galvanised steel gutters — pairing them
  causes fast corrosion.
- Repair corrugated **galvanised iron** with galvanised steel, fixed and flashed the
  traditional way (lapped sheets, domed slotted screws, lead washers). Surface rust
  alone does not mean a roof needs replacing — rust converters plus maintenance
  prolong the original.
- **Ferrous fixings bedded in masonry** (window bars, palisade fixings, cramps)
  corrode in damp masonry and expand, spalling the surrounding stone or brick. Lead
  sleeving at the junction protects against this.

## Traditional fabric that is better than it looks

These are routinely discounted or stripped out unnecessarily — assess their inherent
capability before recommending upgrades:

- **Seasoned hardwood framing** has high inherent fire resistance; it chars slowly and
  has performed better than iron or steel in real fires. Old timber is well seasoned
  and often stronger than it looks.
- **Shellac** finishes give timber some fire protection.
- **Sand-and-lime pugging** and lath-and-plaster provide useful sound and thermal
  insulation.
- If old timber genuinely needs strengthening, **add supplementary members and leave
  the originals in place** rather than replacing them.

## Cracks and movement

Monitor cracks over time before acting. They are usually one of:

- **Stationary** — stable for years; often no action needed.
- **Cyclical** — opening and closing with ground moisture in clay soils; treat by
  stabilising ground moisture, often with no work to the building itself.
- **Progressive** — actively widening; needs assessment by an engineer experienced in
  traditional structures (ordinary engineers may over-cautiously condemn sound old
  buildings).
